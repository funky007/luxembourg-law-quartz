# Caution Dirigeante (Director as Surety)

> **Type**: Specific Surety Category
> **Source**: Westendorf, T.3, §§537, 580, 621
> **Key Consideration**: Informed surety status; corporate benefit

---

## Definition

A *caution dirigeante* is a director, manager, or officer of a company who provides a personal guarantee for the company's obligations. This is extremely common in banking practice, particularly for SME lending where banks require personal commitment from principals.

---

## Particular Characteristics

### Informed Surety (*Caution avertie*)

Director-sureties are generally classified as "informed" (*averti*) rather than "non-informed" (*non averti*) because:

1. **Access to information**: They have full access to the company's financial situation
2. **Professional capacity**: They act in a commercial context
3. **Decision-making role**: They are the ones seeking credit for the company

This classification affects the scope of the bank's duty to warn (*obligation de mise en garde*). Banks owe reduced disclosure duties to informed sureties (Westendorf, T.3, §621).

### Cessation of Functions

A critical question arises when the director ceases their functions: does the guarantee terminate?

**General rule**: The guarantee continues even after cessation of functions unless:
- The contract expressly provides for termination on leaving office
- The surety validly resiles from an open-ended guarantee (*cautionnement omnibus*)

The director's status is considered a *subjective motive* (*cause subjective*) rather than an objective condition of the guarantee. Its disappearance does not affect the guarantee's validity (Westendorf, T.3, §580).

> **Key Point**: Directors who wish their guarantee to terminate when they leave must negotiate an express clause to that effect, or ensure they have the right to terminate prospectively.

---

## Formation Considerations

### Validity

The director's guarantee is subject to normal formation requirements. Particular attention should be paid to:

- **Conflict of interest**: The director acts both for the company (obtaining credit) and personally (providing guarantee)
- **Proportionality**: The guarantee should be proportionate to the director's personal financial capacity
- **Understanding**: Despite being "informed," directors may not fully appreciate personal liability implications

### Art. 1326 Requirements

For guarantees under private signature, the handwritten mention requirement applies. Directors cannot claim commercial exception merely because they act professionally — the exception applies only to *commercial* sureties in the strict sense (Westendorf, T.3, §678).

---

## Effects on Director

### During Company Solvency

The director-surety benefits from the normal surety defenses:
- Right to require the creditor pursue the debtor first (*bénéfice de discussion*) unless waived
- Right to division among co-sureties (*bénéfice de division*) unless waived
- Subrogation rights against the debtor upon payment

In practice, banks systematically require waiver of these benefits.

### Upon Company Insolvency

When the company becomes insolvent:
- The guarantee becomes immediately enforceable (acceleration)
- The director cannot invoke the moratorium granted to the company
- Payment by the director creates a subordinated claim in the insolvency

This creates the uncomfortable situation where directors who managed the company into insolvency must personally bear the consequences.

---

## Duty to Warn (*Mise en garde*)

### Traditional Position

Banks owed minimal disclosure duties to director-sureties who were presumed to know the company's situation better than the bank.

### Evolution

French jurisprudence (influential in Luxembourg) has created uncertainty. Some decisions held that even director-sureties deserve warning if:
- The credit is manifestly disproportionate to the company's capacity
- The director, while "informed" about the company, may be "non-informed" about their personal risk

### Current Approach

The duty to warn focuses on two elements (Westendorf, T.3, §635):
1. The surety's personal financial capacity relative to the guarantee
2. The debtor's financial capacity relative to the guaranteed obligations

For director-sureties, banks have argued that directors know both factors. However, prudent practice suggests documenting:
- The director's awareness of the company's financial position
- The director's understanding of their personal exposure
- Any warnings provided about risk of personal liability

---

## Banking Application

### Documentation

**Guarantee forms** should include:
- Clear identification of the director's personal (not representative) capacity
- Express waiver of *bénéfice de discussion* and *division*
- Acknowledgment of access to company financial information
- Acknowledgment of personal financial capacity to bear the guarantee

**Board resolutions**: The director guaranteeing should not vote on the resolution accepting the guarantee (conflict management).

### Best Practices

1. **Separate advice**: Encourage (or require for large guarantees) the director to seek independent legal advice
2. **Cooling-off period**: Allow time between presenting the guarantee and signature
3. **Financial disclosure**: Obtain the director's personal financial statement
4. **Spousal consent**: Where applicable, obtain spouse's consent (Art. 215 considerations for community property)

### Exit Provisions

Consider including provisions for:
- Termination of prospective coverage upon cessation of functions
- Release upon sale of shares (with bank consent)
- Cap or reduction mechanisms linked to outstanding debt

---

> **Common Law Comparison**
>
> **English Law**: Director guarantees are standard in SME lending. The *Etridge* guidelines require lenders to ensure the guarantor receives independent advice when there are "non-commercial" relationships, but this typically applies to spouses/cohabitants rather than directors.
>
> **US Law**: Director guarantees are common. UCC and state law apply. FDIC lending regulations require specific documentation of personal guarantees.
>
> **Key Similarity**: Both civil and common law systems recognize directors as sophisticated parties deserving less protection than consumer guarantors.
