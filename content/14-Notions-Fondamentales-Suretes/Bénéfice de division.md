# Bénéfice de Division

> **Type**: Surety Defense (Multiple Sureties)
> **Source**: Westendorf, T.3, §§785-810
> **Key Articles**: C.civ. arts. 2025-2026

---

## Definition

The *bénéfice de division* (benefit of division) allows a co-surety, when multiple sureties guarantee the same debt, to require the creditor to divide their claim and pursue each surety only for their proportionate share. It protects co-sureties from bearing more than their fair share.

> **Art. 2026 C.civ.**: "Lorsque plusieurs personnes se sont rendues cautions d'un même débiteur pour une même dette, elles sont obligées chacune à toute la dette."
>
> *Translation*: "When several persons have become sureties for the same debtor for the same debt, each of them is bound for the whole debt."

However, Article 2026 continues: each may invoke division unless they have expressly waived it.

---

## Requirements

For the benefit to apply (Westendorf, T.3, §§790-795):

1. **Multiple sureties**: Two or more persons guaranteeing the same debt
2. **Same debt**: The sureties must guarantee the identical obligation
3. **Same debtor**: They must guarantee the same principal debtor
4. **No waiver**: The benefit must not have been waived

The benefit must be invoked affirmatively by the surety when pursued.

---

## Effect

When properly invoked, the creditor's claim is divided among solvent co-sureties at the time of division. The creditor may only recover from each surety their proportionate share.

**Example**: Three sureties guarantee €300,000. If all are solvent, each may be pursued for only €100,000.

**Insolvency adjustment**: If one co-surety is insolvent, the division occurs among the remaining solvent sureties. The invoking surety does not bear the insolvent's share automatically — this becomes relevant for contribution claims between sureties.

---

## Exclusions and Waivers

### Express Waiver

The benefit is routinely waived in commercial guarantees:
- "Chaque caution renonce au bénéfice de division..."
- "Les cautions s'engagent indivisiblement..."

### Joint and Several Suretyship (*Cautionnement solidaire entre cautions*)

When sureties are bound jointly and severally among themselves (*solidarité entre cofidéjusseurs*), the *bénéfice de division* is excluded. Each surety may be pursued for the entire debt.

### Separate Guarantees

If sureties give separate, independent guarantees (rather than co-signing one guarantee), arguments exist that they are not truly "co-sureties" entitled to division. However, if they guarantee the same debt of the same debtor, the better view is that division remains available (Westendorf, T.3, §800).

---

## Contribution Between Sureties

After paying, a surety may seek contribution from co-sureties for their share. This right arises from:
1. **Legal subrogation** into the creditor's rights
2. **Direct action** between co-sureties based on the relationship between them

The surety who paid may claim from each co-surety their proportionate share plus interest and costs (Westendorf, T.3, §805).

---

## Banking Application

### Standard Documentation

Bank guarantees typically include:
- Express waiver of *bénéfice de division*
- Joint and several liability clause (*solidarité*)
- Indivisibility (*indivisibilité*) language

### Practical Effect

In commercial banking, the benefit is effectively eliminated by standard drafting. The bank may pursue any surety for the full amount, leaving contribution claims to be sorted among the sureties themselves.

For consumer transactions or less sophisticated guarantors, the benefit may still provide meaningful protection if not properly waived.

---

> **Common Law Comparison**
>
> **English Law**: No equivalent default right. Co-sureties are jointly and severally liable unless the guarantee provides otherwise. Contribution rights exist after payment but no right to force division at the enforcement stage.
>
> **UCC (US)**: Similar to English law. Co-sureties may have contribution rights but cannot force the creditor to divide their claim.
>
> **Key Difference**: Luxembourg's default rule protects co-sureties from bearing disproportionate liability; common law leaves this to contractual negotiation.
