# Proportionnalité (Cautionnement)

> **Type**: Validity/Enforceability Doctrine
> **Source**: Westendorf, T.3, §§620-650
> **Key Consideration**: Consumer protection; banking duty

---

## Definition

The principle of proportionality (*proportionnalité*) in suretyship requires that a guarantee should be proportionate to the surety's financial capacity. A manifestly disproportionate guarantee may be unenforceable or give rise to creditor liability.

---

## Origins

The proportionality principle developed primarily in French law and has been influential in Luxembourg. It addresses the concern that unsophisticated guarantors may be induced to provide guarantees far exceeding their ability to pay, creating illusory security for the creditor and ruinous consequences for the surety.

---

## Application in Luxembourg

### General Principle

Luxembourg has not enacted specific proportionality legislation comparable to French consumer law provisions. However, the courts may apply proportionality analysis through:

1. **Duty to warn** (*obligation de mise en garde*): The creditor's failure to warn of manifest disproportion may constitute fault
2. **Good faith**: Enforcement of a manifestly disproportionate guarantee may be contrary to good faith (Art. 1134 al. 3)
3. **Abuse of right**: Enforcing against a surety who clearly could never pay may constitute abuse

### Factors Considered

When assessing proportionality (Westendorf, T.3, §§630-635):

| Factor | Consideration |
|--------|---------------|
| **Surety's assets** | Net worth at time of guarantee |
| **Surety's income** | Regular earnings and capacity |
| **Guarantee amount** | Total potential exposure including interest |
| **Surety's other obligations** | Existing debts and commitments |
| **Future expectations** | Reasonably foreseeable changes |

---

## Consequence of Disproportion

### Creditor Liability

The bank may be liable in damages for loss caused by accepting a manifestly disproportionate guarantee without warning. Damages are typically assessed as "loss of chance" — the chance the surety would not have committed if properly warned.

### Reduction of Guarantee

Some jurisdictions allow reduction of the guarantee to a proportionate amount. Luxembourg courts may have discretion to limit enforcement where disproportion is manifest and the creditor acted in bad faith.

### Not Automatic Nullity

Disproportion alone does not render the guarantee void. The surety must typically show:
- Manifest disproportion at the time of signature
- Creditor knowledge or duty to know
- Absence of adequate warning
- Resulting damage

---

## Informed vs. Non-Informed Sureties

### Non-Informed Sureties (*Cautions non averties*)

Greater protection applies:
- Bank must assess proportionality
- Duty to warn of risks
- Liability for manifestly excessive guarantees

### Informed Sureties (*Cautions averties*)

Reduced protection:
- Presumed to understand their commitment
- Less duty to warn
- Director-sureties typically in this category

However, even informed sureties may benefit from proportionality principles if the disproportion is extreme (Westendorf, T.3, §640).

---

## Assessment Timing

Proportionality is assessed at the **time of the guarantee**, not at enforcement. Subsequent deterioration of the surety's financial position is irrelevant — the surety took on that risk.

However, for continuing guarantees (*cautionnements omnibus*), each increase in exposure may require fresh proportionality assessment.

---

## Banking Application

### Due Diligence

Banks should:
1. **Obtain financial information**: Request surety's statement of assets and liabilities
2. **Assess capacity**: Compare guarantee amount to net worth/income
3. **Document analysis**: Record the proportionality assessment
4. **Warn if disproportionate**: Provide clear warning of risks

### Documentation

Guarantee forms may include:
- Surety's declaration of financial capacity
- Acknowledgment of understanding the risks
- Confirmation of voluntary commitment
- Representation that the guarantee is proportionate to means

### Risk Indicators

Be cautious with:
- Guarantees exceeding surety's net worth
- Guarantees from persons with minimal assets
- Guarantees for amounts vastly exceeding the surety's stake in the transaction
- Consumer guarantees for business debts

---

> **Common Law Comparison**
>
> **English Law**: No general proportionality doctrine. The doctrine of unconscionability may apply in extreme cases but is rarely successful. The focus is on procedural fairness (understanding the transaction) rather than substantive proportionality.
>
> **US Law**: Some states have specific surety protections. Federal regulations (Regulation Z) provide cooling-off periods for certain consumer transactions. Unconscionability doctrine provides limited protection.
>
> **Key Difference**: Civil law's proportionality doctrine provides substantive protection based on the guarantee's terms, while common law focuses primarily on procedural safeguards.
