# Societe par Actions Simplifiee (SAS)

> **Type**: Special Company Form
> **Source**: Steichen, *Precis de droit des societes*, Section 2, n 1079-1105
> **Key Legislation**: Loi du 10 aout 2016; Art. 500-1 et s. L1915

---

## 1. Overview & History

### 1.1 Introduction

**Steichen, n 1079**: The SAS was introduced into Luxembourg law by the Law of 10 August 2016 with the aim of strengthening the attractiveness of the Luxembourg legal framework, particularly in the face of competition from Dutch law. It illustrates particularly well the phenomenon of internationalization of company law. Modeled on French law, the SAS is a commercial company by form that presents three essential characteristics.

**Steichen, n 1079**: The SAS is a distinct form of company from the societe anonyme (SA). It is not a simplified SA, but its regime is defined by reference to the rules applicable to the SA on a supplementary basis. This reference does not operate in all areas, as the legislator excludes the reference in certain domains.

### 1.2 Key Characteristics

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Legal Nature** | Distinct company form, not a simplified SA |
| **Liability** | Limited to contributions (mise determinee) |
| **Minimum Capital** | EUR 1 (same as SA rules apply) |
| **Key Advantage** | Contractual freedom and organizational flexibility |
| **Members** | Called "associes" not "actionnaires" |
| **Public Offering** | Prohibited (Art. 500-2 L1915) |

### 1.3 Essential Characteristics

**Steichen, n 1080**: Very significant derogations can be made to the SA reference rules, since the organization of the SAS is, for the most part, left to **contractual freedom** (*liberte contractuelle*). The limitation of mandatory provisions applicable to SAs should constitute the main attraction of the SAS, which largely escapes the rigidities of the SA.

**Steichen, n 1080**: As a corollary to contractual freedom, the SAS gives strong place to the **intuitu personae** that must predominate among its members. It is therefore symptomatic that the legislator speaks of "*associes*" and not "*actionnaires*."

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Founders

**Steichen, n 1081**: The SAS can be formed by one or more persons. It follows that both natural persons and legal entities can participate in its creation. Likewise, the SAS can have only one shareholder. The single-member SAS (*SASU* - Societe par Actions Simplifiee Unipersonnelle) can be very practical in corporate groups where a parent company holds all the shares of its subsidiary.

**Steichen, n 1081**: Despite the personal character of the SAS, the law does not set a maximum number of shareholders, which could, for example, be useful in companies grouping the employees of a business.

### 2.2 Capital Requirements

**Steichen, n 1082**: In the silence of the law concerning the minimum share capital of the SAS, the rules of the SA are applicable, which includes the rule of fixed capital (*fixite du capital*). It is therefore not possible to create a SAS with variable capital.

**Steichen, n 1082**: The minimum share capital can result from contributions in cash (*apports en numeraire*) or in kind (*apports en nature*), but not from contributions in services (*apports en industrie*). In case of contributions in kind, the rules relating thereto in the case of contributions to SAs will be applicable.

**Steichen, n 1082**: The share capital can be represented by all types of shares already studied: ordinary shares, preference shares, etc. The rules regarding capital release are also the same as those applicable to SAs.

### 2.3 Prohibition on Public Offerings

**Steichen, n 1082**: As the SAS presents, unlike the SA, a closed character, the SAS cannot make a public offering of shares. However, this does not prevent it from transforming into an SA when it wants to place its shares with the public. Similarly, if the SAS has SAs as shareholders, these can themselves make public calls for savings and subscribe to the capital of the SAS by means of the funds thus collected. Finally, nothing prevents it from issuing bonds to the public, as long as they are not convertible into shares.

### 2.4 Transformation into SAS

**Steichen, n 1083**: An existing company can be transformed into a SAS. The original draft law provided for unanimity for such transformation. The reason for the unanimity requirement was the concern to avoid that shareholders of an SA, for example, impose excessively strong ties regarding their status or display too significant a decline in their capacity to influence the management of the SAS. This proposed text was ultimately not retained, as the Legal Commission considered it unnecessary to subject the transformation of a company into a SAS to other formalities or conditions than those provided for in articles 1010-1 et seq. of the 1915 law.

## 3. Governance

### 3.1 General Principles

**Steichen, n 1085**: It is at this level that contractual freedom and legal ingenuity take on their full importance. There is, in fact, no legal organization of powers as in the SA, the law explicitly excluding articles relating to the management of SAs from the supplementary rules applicable to the SAS.

**Steichen, n 1085**: In the absence of mandatory and supplementary provisions on this point, it is for the articles of association (*statuts*) to exhaustively define the mode of operation of corporate management. The same remark can be made regarding decisions to be taken by the shareholders. Drafting the articles of a SAS therefore requires certain legal expertise.

### 3.2 The President

**Steichen, n 1086**: According to Article 500-4 L1915, "the company is represented with respect to third parties and in court, either as plaintiff or defendant, by a president designated under the conditions provided for by the articles of association." **Sole mandatory organ** of the SAS, the president is also **unique**, co-presidency being excluded. The use of the singular to designate the president commands this interpretation.

**Steichen, n 1087**: The appointment of the president is made in accordance with the conditions set by the articles. He may therefore be chosen from among the shareholders as well as from third parties; be appointed by the shareholders in a meeting; by a predetermined shareholder outside of any meeting; by a committee of external parties; etc. The articles will also freely define the duration of his functions as well as the modalities of his dismissal.

**Steichen, n 1087**: The articles may provide for entirely free dismissal, absolute irrevocability of the president, dismissal with or without cause, with or without compensation, etc. Dismissal can be fixed in the articles as requiring a simple majority, a reinforced majority, with or without quorum.

### 3.3 Powers of the President

**Steichen, n 1088**: The powers of the president in the external order are modeled on those of the board of directors of the SA, in that the president represents the SAS vis-a-vis third parties. Any limitation of powers resulting from the articles will be unenforceable against third parties, in the sense that the president binds the SAS for everything that falls within the corporate purpose, even if the articles prohibit the president from performing certain acts of corporate management without the consent of the shareholders.

**Steichen, n 1088**: In the internal order, it is for the articles to fix the powers that the president will have. The law as such confers no power on him, so the articles could, for example, provide that the president should obtain the consent of the shareholders for any act of corporate management.

**Steichen, n 1088**: The president can be flanked by a whole range of other management and control bodies, whose existence is optional and whose denomination is variable. The president may have all powers, certain ones alone, or share them with directors, or even have no power. The powers of the president can therefore, over the entire range, extend from the figurehead president to the omnipotent president.

### 3.4 Directors (Directeurs)

**Steichen, n 1089**: According to Article 500-4 L1915, it is for the articles to fix the conditions under which the company is managed. The shareholders therefore have very great freedom, subject to the designation of a president to represent the company.

**Steichen, n 1089**: The possibility of entrusting corporate management to a person other than the president results from Article 500-4. This provision allows the articles to regulate the conditions under which the exercise of the general and permanent power to represent and bind the company, of which the president is the depository by principle, can also be attributed to the general manager (*directeur general*) or deputy general manager (*directeur general delegue*).

**Steichen, n 1089**: It will be for the articles to define the organization of decision-making in the presence of a plurality of directors. They may function as a body, taking their decisions unanimously, by qualified or simple majority; but it will also be possible to entrust each of the directors with the entire corporate management or to distinguish according to the directors concerned depending on the matter concerned.

### 3.5 Responsibility of Directors

**Steichen, n 1092**: The president and directors are agents (*mandataires*) of the company, so they incur no personal obligation when acting on behalf of the company. The responsibilities of the president and directors of the SAS are those of the directors and members of the management board in the SA (Art. 500-6 L1915).

### 3.6 Conflicts of Interest

**Steichen, n 1091**: The question of conflicts of interest (ordinary operations concluded under normal conditions are not covered: Art. 500-8 para. 4 L1915) is regulated in a manner analogous to what happens in SAs. If the president is alone, or if he is surrounded by directors who also have a conflict of interest, the operation will be mentioned in minutes and the shareholders will be subsequently informed of the conflict. If the conflict of interest concerns only the directors, the decision will be taken by the president.

## 4. Collective Decisions of Shareholders

### 4.1 Statutory vs. Legal Competence

**Steichen, n 1093**: Subject to certain decisions requiring, by law, to be taken collectively by the shareholders, the articles have complete latitude to determine the decisions that will fall to the shareholders. Shareholder meetings therefore have a different place in a SAS than in an SA. They are in fact optional, except in two cases:
1. Shareholder meetings may be provided for by the **articles**
2. Shareholder meetings may be **required by law**

**Steichen, n 1094**: Article 500-7 para. 2 L1915 provides that decisions that must be adopted in a meeting include matters relating to: capital increases, redemptions or reductions; mergers; demergers; dissolution; transformation into another company form; appointment of auditors; annual accounts; and profits.

**Steichen, n 1094**: It follows *a contrario* from this list that the appointment and dismissal of the president and directors, the issuance of loans including bond issues, amendments to the articles in general and to the corporate purpose in particular do not fall within the domain mandatorily reserved for shareholders.

### 4.2 Decision-Making Procedures

**Steichen, n 1095**: Complete freedom is left to the articles regarding the definition of the modalities according to which decisions will be taken. The mode of consultation is therefore freely determined by the articles: written consultation or physical presence, personal presence or possibility of representation; participation by videoconference; etc.

**Steichen, n 1095**: The articles will also determine the persons competent to convene meetings: the president; the directors; the shareholders; a shareholder; a third party; etc.

### 4.3 Quorum and Majority

**Steichen, n 1096**: The law is silent on the quorum and majority conditions for collective decision-making. The articles are therefore free to fix the quorum of their choice; in the absence of articles, no quorum is required. The majority rules are also freely defined in the articles: absolute majority; reinforced; unanimity, and why not decision-making on the basis of a minority vote.

### 4.4 Voting Rights

**Steichen, n 1096**: Regarding the voting right attached to each share, the reference to SA rules is not automatic: it only operates to the extent compatible with the SAS. As the modulation of voting rights constitutes an important aspect of the flexibility of company law, and as Article 500-1 para. 3 L1915 excludes voting power limitation rules from the reference applicable to SASs, the legislator intended to exclude all the rules known in respect of SAs when it comes to defining voting rights modalities in SASs.

**Steichen, n 1096**: It can therefore be held that **voting rights may be freely modulated** in the SAS. The articles of the SAS can therefore perfectly create shares with plural voting rights (double, quintuple, etc.), provide for voting right suspensions as a sanction, etc.

## 5. Share Transfers

### 5.1 Restriction Clauses

**Steichen, n 1097**: The draft law provided for five clauses that the authors proposed to expressly enshrine in the SAS: approval (*agrement*), inalienability (*inalienabilite*), withdrawal (*retrait*), and exclusion. The draft law intended that the law grant them maximum effectiveness by voiding any transfer made in violation of the articles.

**Steichen, n 1097**: The Legal Commission did not retain these proposed texts. The suppression was justified by the fact that the various clauses could, in any case, exist in SAs, and a fortiori, to an even greater extent, in SASs. Only the sanction for transfers made in violation of clauses limiting the transferability of shares was ultimately introduced into law.

**Steichen, n 1097**: Article 500-9 L1915: "Any transfer of shares made in violation of the statutory clauses is **null**." The possible good faith of the third-party acquirer will not matter, since the statutory provisions are also enforceable against him. The third-party acquirer must therefore return the shares acquired in violation of the articles.

## 6. Control and Audit

### 6.1 Statutory Auditor

**Steichen, n 1098**: The SAS is mandatorily subject to control by an auditor (*commissaire*) or approved statutory auditor (*reviseur d'entreprises agree*), depending on the criteria set by law. The auditor is mandatorily chosen on the basis of a collective decision of the shareholders. His mission in the SAS is the same as in the SA, and he will incur the same responsibilities.

### 6.2 Additional Controls

**Steichen, n 1098**: The shareholders of the SAS may, as in any company, request the court to appoint a special commissioner for the purpose of verifying certain operations. The SAS may also function on the basis of the SA with management board model; the articles may provide for a management board (*directoire*) controlled by a supervisory board (*conseil de surveillance*).

**Steichen, n 1098**: Specialized committees (nomination, remuneration, etc.) may be entrusted with specific missions. Under penalty of being treated as "de facto managers," persons responsible for control must limit themselves to this aspect and not encroach on the action of directors and the president.

---

## 7. Dissolution

**Steichen, n 1084**: The SA regime applies to the dissolution of the SAS. As a result, the common law causes of dissolution are supplemented by causes specific to SAs, relating to the loss of a significant part of the share capital.

**Steichen, n 1084**: Being able to be single-member and having no upper limit in terms of number of shareholders, the SAS is not threatened by variations affecting the number of its shareholders. When it has only one shareholder left, the SAS becomes a SASU and continues to exist as before.

## Banking & Finance Application

The SAS is increasingly used in banking and finance structures for several key reasons:

1. **Holding Structures**: The SASU (single-shareholder SAS) can serve as an intermediate holding company in group structures, creating a "Russian doll" system where a parent company holds its subsidiary through a SASU.

2. **Joint Ventures**: The flexibility in governance arrangements makes the SAS ideal for joint ventures where parties want to negotiate bespoke management and decision-making structures without the rigidity of SA requirements.

3. **Private Equity**: The ability to create shares with variable voting rights and to implement robust transfer restriction clauses makes the SAS attractive for private equity structures where control mechanisms are critical.

4. **Management Participation**: The SAS allows for flexible arrangements regarding management participation and incentive structures, as the mandatory organs are minimal (only president required).

5. **Investment Funds**: Although the SAS cannot make public offerings of shares, it can issue bonds publicly (if not convertible to shares), making it suitable for certain fund structures.

**Key limitations for finance**: The prohibition on public share offerings means the SAS is unsuitable as a vehicle for listed companies or UCITS. It also cannot be used for banking or insurance entities that require specific corporate forms.
