# Cautionnement — Formation

> **Type**: Personal Security (*sûreté personnelle*)
> **Source**: Westendorf, T.3, §§500-650
> **Key Articles**: C.civ. arts. 2011-2020, 1108, 1326

---

## Definition

Suretyship (*cautionnement*) is a contract by which a person (the surety, *caution*) undertakes towards a creditor (*créancier*) to perform the obligation of the principal debtor (*débiteur principal*) if the latter fails to do so. It is a personal security creating a secondary obligation dependent on the principal debt.

> **Art. 2011 C.civ.**: "Celui qui se rend caution d'une obligation se soumet envers le créancier à satisfaire à cette obligation, si le débiteur n'y satisfait pas lui-même."
>
> *Translation*: "A person who stands surety for an obligation binds himself towards the creditor to satisfy that obligation if the debtor does not satisfy it himself."

---

## General Contract Validity (Art. 1108)

The validity of a suretyship contract depends on the four classic conditions of Article 1108 C.civ.: consent, capacity, object, and cause. Each has particular applications in the suretyship context.

### 1. Consent (*Consentement*)

The surety's consent must be free from defects (*vices du consentement*).

#### Error (*Erreur*)

Error may vitiate consent if it is substantial. Key distinctions (Westendorf, T.3, §§508-520):

**Error on the debtor's solvency** (*erreur sur la solvabilité*): Generally not a ground for nullity unless the creditor made specific representations. The surety assumes the risk of the debtor's insolvency — that is precisely the purpose of the guarantee (Westendorf, T.3, §514).

**Error on the existence of other securities**: May constitute grounds for nullity if the surety can prove that the existence of other guarantees was a determining factor (*cause déterminante*) of the engagement, and the creditor knew or should have known this (Westendorf, T.3, §517).

> **Common Law Comparison**
>
> Luxembourg law follows the classic civil law approach requiring the error to be *substantial* (going to the essence of the contract). Unlike English law's doctrine of misrepresentation, there is no general right to rescind for innocent misrepresentation. The surety must prove the error was *excusable* and *determinative*.

#### Fraud (*Dol*)

Fraud by the creditor may nullify the suretyship. However, fraud by the principal debtor (*dol du débiteur principal*) is generally not imputable to the creditor unless the creditor was complicit. The surety cannot invoke against the creditor the fraud of a third party (Westendorf, T.3, §§540-560).

Key banking implication: A bank's failure to disclose the debtor's financial difficulties does not constitute fraud unless there was active concealment (*réticence dolosive*) in circumstances creating a duty to disclose.

#### Duress (*Violence*)

Economic duress (*violence économique*) may vitiate consent but requires:
- Illegitimate constraint or exploitation of weakness
- Absence of reasonable alternatives
- The constraint must be the determining cause of the engagement

The mere existence of economic pressure from the bank-client relationship is insufficient (Westendorf, T.3, §§570-575).

---

### 2. Capacity (*Capacité*)

Suretyship is an act of disposition (*acte de disposition*) of particular gravity because the surety may not be immediately aware of the full extent of their potential liability (Westendorf, T.3, §522).

**Minors**: Cannot validly act as surety alone. The question whether a legal representative can bind a minor as surety (even with judicial authorization) remains debated, though recent jurisprudence suggests it may be possible if in the minor's interest (Westendorf, T.3, §523).

**Adults under guardianship** (*tutelle*): Acts are void by operation of law after the judgment opening the guardianship (Art. 502 C.civ.).

**Adults under curatorship** (*curatelle*): May act as surety with the curator's assistance (Art. 510 C.civ.).

**Mental incapacity** (*insanité d'esprit*): Under Art. 489 C.civ., a suretyship may be annulled if the surety proves they suffered from a mental disorder at the time of the act. The burden of proof lies on the party alleging incapacity.

---

### 3. Object (*Objet*)

The object of the surety's obligation is the debt of the principal debtor. It must be:
- **Existent or future** (future debts are permissible)
- **Licit** (guarantee of criminal fines is void)
- **Determined or determinable** (Westendorf, T.3, §§530-532)

#### Future Debts (*Dettes futures*)

A suretyship may validly guarantee future debts provided the debtor is identifiable and the guaranteed debts are determinable. This is the basis for the "*cautionnement omnibus*" — a general guarantee of all present and future debts of a debtor to a specific creditor (Westendorf, T.3, §§536-540).

> **Art. 1129 C.civ.** requires the object to be determined or determinable — not that it must exist at the time of contracting.

---

### 4. Cause

The cause of the surety's obligation is a complex and debated topic. Luxembourg jurisprudence recognizes both subjective and objective dimensions (Westendorf, T.3, §§574-580).

#### Objective Cause

Following the French *Lempereur* approach, the cause of the surety's obligation is found in the credit or advantage that the creditor provides to the principal debtor. The guarantee is void for lack of cause if no such advantage was provided (Westendorf, T.3, §578).

> "La cause objective du cautionnement étant l'avantage escompté par le débiteur, l'absence de cause paraît devoir coïncider nécessairement avec l'existence ou la nullité de l'obligation garantie."
>
> *Translation*: "The objective cause of the suretyship being the advantage expected by the debtor, absence of cause must necessarily coincide with the existence or nullity of the guaranteed obligation."

#### Subjective Cause

The surety's personal motives (e.g., family relationship with the debtor, director status) are generally irrelevant unless expressly made conditions of the engagement. The disappearance of the subjective cause (e.g., director ceasing functions) does not terminate the suretyship (Westendorf, T.3, §580).

## Corporate Benefit (*Intérêt Social*)

When a company acts as surety for another's debt, the guarantee must be analyzed for conformity with the corporate interest. This is critical for upstream, downstream, and cross-stream guarantees in group structures.

### Companies with Limited Liability (*Sociétés à risque limité*)

For SA, SàRL, and SAS companies, a guarantee contrary to the corporate interest is **not** per se void. The company is bound by acts of its directors/managers even if exceeding the corporate purpose, unless the third party knew or could not have been unaware that the act exceeded that purpose (Art. 441-13, 710-15 of the 1915 Companies Law; Westendorf, T.3, §610).

This follows the EU First Company Law Directive (now codified in Directive 2017/1132) protecting third parties dealing with companies.

### Companies with Unlimited Liability (*Sociétés à risque illimité*)

For partnerships and SCIs, stricter scrutiny applies. A guarantee may be void if:
1. It engages substantially all of the company's assets
2. There is no adequate counterpart or group benefit
3. It compromises the very existence of the company

The test requires proportionality between the guarantee and the benefit to the guarantor company (Westendorf, T.3, §611).

### Group Interest (*Intérêt du groupe*)

The concept of group interest may justify guarantees that lack direct benefit to the guarantor if:
- There is genuine economic integration within the group
- The guarantee serves the survival or benefit of the group as a whole
- The guarantor indirectly benefits from the group's prosperity

This concept is particularly important for holding company structures common in Luxembourg (Westendorf, T.3, §611).

## Formal Requirements

### Express Character (Art. 2015)

> **Art. 2015 C.civ.**: "Le cautionnement ne se présume point ; il doit être exprès, et on ne peut pas l'étendre au-delà des limites dans lesquelles il a été contracté."
>
> *Translation*: "Suretyship is not presumed; it must be express, and cannot be extended beyond the limits within which it was contracted."

The suretyship must be express but need not follow any particular form. The requirement serves interpretive purposes: in case of doubt, the guarantee is interpreted restrictively in favor of the surety (Westendorf, T.3, §533).

### Written Evidence (Art. 1326)

For suretyships under private signature (*sous seing privé*), Art. 1326 C.civ. requires a handwritten mention (*mention manuscrite*) of the amount in words and figures. This is a rule of proof, not validity:
- Absence renders the document a mere *commencement de preuve par écrit*
- Not required for notarial acts (*actes authentiques*)
- Not required for commercial sureties (*cautionnement commercial*)

## Duty to Warn (*Obligation de mise en garde*)

### Evolution

Luxembourg jurisprudence has evolved significantly on the creditor's duty to warn the surety. The traditional position was minimal: the surety must inform themselves about the debtor's situation (Westendorf, T.3, §616).

### Current Position

Following French jurisprudential developments, Luxembourg courts now recognize a duty to warn (*devoir de mise en garde*) owed by professional creditors (particularly banks) to non-professional sureties (*cautions non averties*). The creditor must warn the surety of the risks of the engagement given:
1. The surety's financial capacity
2. The debtor's financial capacity relative to the guaranteed debt (Westendorf, T.3, §§617-635)

### Informed Sureties (*Cautions averties*)

Director-sureties (*cautions dirigeantes*) are generally considered "informed" and may not benefit from the duty to warn because they have access to all relevant information about the company whose debts they guarantee. However, the personal/professional distinction remains relevant (Westendorf, T.3, §621).

---

> **Common Law Comparison**
>
> **English Law**: No general duty to disclose; *caveat emptor* applies. However, the *Royal Bank of Scotland v Etridge* line requires explanation of risk to spouses/cohabitants providing security for partner's debts.
>
> **UCC (US)**: Article 9 does not specifically address suretyship formation. State law governs suretyship contracts with varying formality requirements. Defenses based on fraud or lack of consideration are broadly similar.
>
> **Key Difference**: Luxembourg civil law imposes a higher standard of good faith (*bonne foi*) in contractual relationships, potentially supporting broader protective duties than common law jurisdictions.

## Banking Application

### Facility Documentation

**Personal Guarantee Forms**: Banks typically use standardized guarantee forms including:
- Clear identification of the guaranteed obligations
- Express waiver of *bénéfice de discussion* and *bénéfice de division*
- Handwritten mention per Art. 1326 for private signature documents
- Clear statement of joint and several liability (*solidarité*)

**Upstream/Downstream Guarantees**: For intra-group guarantees:
- Verify corporate authorization (board resolution, authorized signatories)
- Consider Art. 49-2 (financial assistance) for target company guarantees
- Document the group benefit justification

### Corporate Authorization

For corporate sureties, verify:
1. Powers of the signatory under statutes and law
2. Conformity with corporate purpose (*objet social*)
3. Where prudent, board or shareholder approval
4. Arm's length terms for intra-group guarantees

### CSSF Considerations

While the CSSF does not directly regulate suretyship contracts, banks must:
- Include guarantee exposures in credit risk assessments
- Apply appropriate due diligence to surety financial capacity
- Consider guarantees in large exposure calculations
- Document the credit analysis supporting reliance on guarantees
