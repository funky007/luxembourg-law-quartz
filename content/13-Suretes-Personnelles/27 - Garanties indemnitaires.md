# Garanties Indemnitaires (Indemnity Guarantees)

> **Type**: Non-Accessory Personal Security
> **Source**: Westendorf, T.3, §§1500-1700
> **Key Forms**: Letters of Intent (*lettres d'intention*), Promise to Procure Performance (*porte-fort d'exécution*)

---

## Definition

An indemnity guarantee (*garantie indemnitaire*) is a contract by which a person (the guarantor) undertakes independently to indemnify another person (the beneficiary) in the event that the beneficiary does not receive a performance promised by a third party (Westendorf, T.3, §1500).

The distinguishing feature is that liability arises through **contractual responsibility** — the guarantor's own breach of their undertaking — rather than through an accessory obligation to pay another's debt.

> **Key principle**: The guarantor's obligation is to compensate for loss suffered, not to perform or pay the debtor's obligation itself.

---

## Mechanism

### Contractual Responsibility Framework

The indemnity guarantee functions through the law of contractual responsibility:
1. The guarantor makes a promise (to ensure performance, to procure payment, etc.)
2. If the promise is not fulfilled, the guarantor is in breach
3. The breach triggers liability for damages (*dommages-intérêts*)
4. Damages equal the beneficiary's loss from non-receipt of the promised performance

### Distinction from Other Securities

| Security Type | Obligation | Trigger |
|---------------|------------|---------|
| **Suretyship** | Pay debtor's debt | Debtor's default |
| **Independent guarantee** | Pay guaranteed sum | Compliant demand |
| **Indemnity guarantee** | Compensate loss | Breach of guarantor's promise |

## Letters of Intent (*Lettres d'intention*)

### Definition

A letter of intent (*lettre d'intention*, *lettre de confort*, *lettre de patronage*) is a document by which a person (typically a parent company) makes undertakings to a creditor of another person (typically a subsidiary) regarding the debtor's ability to meet its obligations.

### Spectrum of Commitments

Letters of intent range from weak to strong:

#### Moral/Informational (Non-binding)
- Acknowledgment of awareness of the subsidiary's debt
- Statement of shareholding percentage
- Expression of support policy
- These create no legal obligation

#### Behavioral Commitments (Binding)
- Undertaking to maintain shareholding
- Commitment to ensure adequate capitalization
- Promise to manage the subsidiary prudently
- Promise to "do what is necessary" (*faire le nécessaire*)
- Breach triggers damages liability

#### Result Commitments (Strong)
- Undertaking that the subsidiary *will* perform
- Promise to ensure payment
- Approaches a *porte-fort* (see below)
- Strongest form of comfort letter

### Qualification Challenges

The key challenge is determining whether a letter creates binding obligations. Courts examine:
- Precise wording (commitment vs. mere statement of intent)
- Context and commercial purpose
- Whether specific actions are promised
- Presence of conditions or reservations

> **Jurisprudence**: Luxembourg courts have recognized that even undertakings to "do the necessary" (*faire le nécessaire*) may create binding obligations whose breach sounds in damages.

### Assessment of Damages

If the letter of intent is breached:
- **Means obligation** (*obligation de moyens*): Creditor must prove the guarantor failed to use reasonable efforts
- **Result obligation** (*obligation de résultat*): Breach is established by the debtor's non-performance

Damages typically equal the loss suffered from non-payment, potentially the full amount of the unpaid debt plus consequential losses.

## Porte-Fort d'Exécution

### Definition

A *porte-fort d'exécution* (promise to procure performance) is an undertaking that a third party will perform their obligations. If the third party does not perform, the promisor is liable in damages.

> **Distinguished from**: *Porte-fort de ratification* — a promise that a third party will ratify a contract. The ratification *porte-fort* is not a security; it addresses whether a contract will bind a third party.

### Legal Basis

The *porte-fort* derives from Article 1120 C.civ.:

> **Art. 1120 C.civ.**: "Néanmoins on peut se porter fort pour un tiers, en promettant le fait de celui-ci ; sauf l'indemnité contre celui qui s'est porté fort ou qui a promis de faire ratifier, si le tiers refuse de tenir l'engagement."
>
> *Translation*: "Nevertheless, one may promise the act of a third party; subject to indemnity against the promisor, if the third party refuses to fulfill the undertaking."

### Effect

When a *porte-fort d'exécution* is given:
- If the debtor performs → no liability
- If the debtor defaults → promisor liable for damages
- Damages = creditor's loss from non-performance

### Distinction from Suretyship

| | Suretyship | Porte-fort d'exécution |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Accessory | Independent |
| **Obligation** | Pay debtor's debt | Compensate for debtor's non-performance |
| **Measure** | Amount of debt | Damages (may differ from debt) |
| **Defenses** | May raise debtor's defenses | Cannot raise debtor's defenses |
| **Subrogation** | Legal subrogation rights | No automatic subrogation |

---

## Common Features

### Independence

Both forms of indemnity guarantee are independent of the underlying obligation:
- Validity does not depend on validity of underlying debt
- Defenses available to debtor cannot be raised
- The guarantor's own promise is the source of liability

### Damages Basis

Liability is measured by the beneficiary's actual loss, which may be:
- Equal to the unpaid debt
- Greater (if consequential damages proven)
- Lesser (if partial performance or mitigation)

### Proof Requirements

The beneficiary must prove:
1. A binding commitment existed
2. The commitment was breached (debtor did not perform)
3. The loss suffered
4. Causation between breach and loss

---

> **Common Law Comparison**
>
> **English Law**: The distinction between guarantees and indemnities is fundamental. An indemnity is a primary obligation; a guarantee is secondary. English law strictly construes guarantees and applies the rule in *Holme v Brunskill* (material variation discharges guarantor) only to guarantees, not indemnities.
>
> **Comfort Letters**: English courts historically treated most comfort letters as non-binding (*Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining*). More recent cases show willingness to find binding obligations where language is sufficiently committal.
>
> **Key Difference**: Luxembourg courts may be more willing to find binding obligations in comfort letters than English courts, but qualification remains highly fact-dependent.

## Banking Application

### Letters of Intent

**Usage**: Common in group lending structures where the bank requires parent company support but:
- The parent does not wish to give a formal guarantee (balance sheet, regulatory, or commercial reasons)
- The transaction does not warrant full guarantee
- Graduated comfort is commercially appropriate

**Due Diligence**:
- Carefully analyze the letter's language
- Assess enforceability and likely damages recovery
- Consider whether a stronger security is commercially achievable
- Document the bank's understanding of the commitment

**Risk Assessment**:
- Letters of intent provide weaker protection than guarantees
- Enforcement requires proving breach and damages
- Recovery may be less than the full debt amount
- Consider obtaining legal opinion on enforceability

### Porte-Fort

**Usage**: Less common in pure banking but may appear in:
- Acquisition finance (seller's porte-fort regarding target obligations)
- Structured transactions
- Situations where full guarantee is not available

**Documentation**:
- Clearly specify the performance being promised
- Define what constitutes breach
- Consider whether result or means obligation is intended

### Combination Structures

Banks sometimes obtain:
- Formal guarantee (primary security)
- Letter of intent from parent (additional comfort)
- Negative pledge from group companies
- Cross-default provisions

This layered approach provides multiple enforcement routes.
